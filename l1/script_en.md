## B01

Today we revise Lesson One. Temperature, and thermometers.


## B02

First, key terms: temperature, Celsius scale, fixed points, ice point, steam point, liquid-in-glass thermometer, thermometric substance, linear relationship, thermal equilibrium.


## B03

Temperature and heat are often mixed up. One is temperature; the other is heat — two different ideas.


## B04

Temperature tells how hot or cold an object is, and links to the average kinetic energy of its particles. Faster particles mean higher average KE, and higher temperature.


## B05

Heat is energy transfer that happens because of a temperature difference. Heat is not “how much temperature an object contains.”


## B06

The Celsius scale is set by two fixed points. Remember the condition: standard atmospheric pressure.


## B07

The melting point of pure ice — the ice point — is zero degrees Celsius. The boiling point of pure water — the steam point — is one hundred degrees Celsius.


## B08

A liquid-in-glass thermometer uses a thermometric substance, such as mercury or alcohol. Its volume changes with temperature, so the liquid-column length changes too.


## B09

We usually assume a linear relationship between column length and temperature. So between the ice point and the steam point, we divide the scale into one hundred equal parts.


## B10

For an ungraduated liquid-in-glass thermometer under the linear assumption, temperature theta equals L minus L-zero, divided by L-one-hundred minus L-zero, times one hundred. The unit is degrees Celsius. L-zero is the ice-point length; L-one-hundred is the steam-point length; L is the length when you measure.


## B11

If you know the temperature and want the column length: L equals L-zero, plus theta over one hundred, times L-one-hundred minus L-zero.


## B12

Here is a short worked demo. L-zero is two point zero centimetres; L-one-hundred is twenty-two point zero centimetres; measured L is twelve point zero centimetres. Find temperature theta.


## B13

Substitute: twelve point zero minus two point zero, divided by twenty-two point zero minus two point zero, times one hundred. That is ten divided by twenty, times one hundred. So theta equals fifty point zero degrees Celsius. Always include the unit.


## B14

Three common misconceptions. One: not every thermometer is naturally linear — you need fixed-point calibration. Two: a thermometer reading is not the absolute amount of heat or internal energy. Three: do not use body temperature or room temperature as Celsius fixed points.


## B15

Remember four points. Temperature links to average kinetic energy; heat is energy transfer from a temperature difference. Fixed points: zero and one hundred degrees Celsius. Use the linear formula for theta. Demo answer: fifty point zero degrees Celsius.
